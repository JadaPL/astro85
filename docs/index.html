Benzene on Mars

Checking NASA’s chemistry results with the Curiosity rover’s raw data. Every graph on this page is interactive.

How to use the graphs: hover over a line or bar to see its numbers · drag across a graph to zoom in · double-click to zoom back out · click a name in a legend to hide or show that line.

The question

Living things on Earth are built from carbon-based chemicals, which chemists call organic molecules. Mars once had liquid water, so scientists want to know whether its old rocks contain carbon-based chemicals too. Finding them would not mean Mars had life (flour and sugar are ingredients, not a cake), but it tells us whether the raw materials were there.

NASA publishes the rover’s raw measurements online. This project takes one experiment, rebuilds NASA’s results from those raw numbers, and checks whether they match what NASA’s science team reported.

How the rover’s lab works

Curiosity carries a small chemistry lab called SAM (Sample Analysis at Mars). It works in three steps:

1 · Bake
Rock powder goes into a tiny oven. Heating it releases gases.
2 · Sort
The gases race through a long, thin tube, so each chemical comes out at its own time. This is called gas chromatography.
3 · Weigh
Each chemical is broken into pieces that are weighed. The pattern of weights is its fingerprint. This is called mass spectrometry.

The weights are written as m/z numbers. A whole benzene molecule weighs 78, so benzene shows up strongly at m/z 78. Toluene, a close relative of benzene, breaks mostly into a piece that weighs 91.

      Graph 3 · Chemical fingerprints

      What you’re looking at

      Each bar is one weight that a chemical breaks into, and its height is how many pieces of that weight were counted. The top half is what Curiosity measured at a bump. The bottom half, flipped upside down, is the same chemical measured in a lab on Earth by NIST, the US standards agency. If the bars line up, it’s the same chemical. The similarity score runs from 0 (nothing in common) to 1 (the same pattern). Weights below 35 (the gray band) are left out of the score, because air and water are always in the instrument.

      What it tells us

      Benzene (left): the bars line up almost perfectly, a score of 0.99. This bump really is benzene.
      Toluene (right): toluene’s own bars, 91 and 92, are there. But the tallest bars, 15 and 69, are not toluene: a second, unidentified chemical came out of the tube at the same moment, so the score is only 0.42.

      Bonus · Detective work: who is the mystery chemical?

      In Graph 3, toluene’s bump turned out to hide a second chemical, whose biggest pieces weighed 15, 69 and 127. Can the data tell us what it is? Chemists narrow down an unknown chemical with a few standard clues. Here they are, one by one.

      Clue 1: the detector got overloaded

      What you’re looking at: close-ups of a few weights as the mystery chemical comes out of the tube, in both sorting runs. Weights 69 and 127 shoot up together just after toluene (91) and fade together, so they come from the same chemical. Weight 58 is strange: it reads almost zero right when the chemical is strongest, then jumps up a few seconds later. The gray line is the highest reading anywhere in the file (6.7 million counts per second).

      Tube 4, around peak 14 (toluene)

      Tube 1, around peak 4

      What it tells us: a signal that disappears exactly at its peak, in an instrument that tops out like this, looks like detector overload: the signal is too strong for the counter to keep up, so it reports far too little. That means weight 58 is probably the chemical’s strongest piece. So for its fingerprint we use the tail of the bump (912–935 seconds), where the signal is back in the range the detector can count.

      Clue 2: what the pieces say

      The biggest pieces of the clean fingerprint are 58 (100%), 69 (39%), 127 (25%), 78 (8%), 50 (5%).

      ClueWhat the data showsWhat it means
      Heaviest piece127The whole molecule probably weighs 127. An odd weight means it contains nitrogen (the “nitrogen rule”).
      Echo at 1283.7% of 127About 3 carbon atoms (each one adds about 1.1%).
      Echo at 1290.3% of 127No chlorine (would be ~32%), sulfur (~4%) or bromine (~97%).
      Piece 6939% of the biggest pieceThe weight of a CF₃ piece (one carbon, three fluorine): a hint of fluorine.
      Pieces 73 and 750.1% and 0.1%Almost absent, so probably not one of the reagent’s silicon chemicals (they usually make these pieces).
      Tube 1, peak 4126÷127 = 0.22 (mystery 0.21), 78÷127 = 0.28 (mystery 0.31)Same piece ratios, so NASA’s unnamed peak 4 in tube 1 looks like the same chemical.

      A molecule that weighs 127, with about 3 carbon atoms, one nitrogen and fluorine, is a very specific description.

      Clue 3: the suspect lineup

      What you’re looking at: the mystery fingerprint (top) mirrored against the best-matching lab fingerprint (bottom), then the similarity score of every suspect. The suspects are the chemicals the NASA team lists for this experiment, plus chemicals that could explain weights 58 or 127.

      The verdict: the mystery chemical is almost certainly N-methyl-trifluoroacetamide (similarity 0.996; the next best, trifluoroiodomethane (CF3I), scores only 0.30). It is a by-product of MTBSTFA, a lab chemical the rover carries from Earth that had soaked into this rock sample, and the NASA team’s own oven-run table lists it in this experiment. So it most likely came from Earth, not Mars. NASA’s unnamed peak 4 in tube 1 looks like the same chemical.

      How sure is this?

      • The fingerprint match is very strong, and the chemical fits every clue: it weighs 127 and has 3 carbon atoms, one nitrogen and a CF₃ group.
      • “Detector overload” is our explanation for weight 58’s strange dip. It fits the evidence, but this file alone can’t prove it.
      • Weight 15 is bigger than the lab fingerprint predicts. The score ignores weights below 35, and other chemicals can make that small piece too.
      • Three silicon chemicals from the reagent couldn’t be scored, because NIST has no fingerprint for them. Their usual marker pieces (73 and 75) are almost absent, though.

      Lab fingerprints for all the suspects come from the NIST Chemistry WebBook (N-methyl-trifluoroacetamide: CAS 815-06-5). The full working is in section 9 of the project notebook.

        What this does not show

        • Not proof of life. Carbon-based chemicals also form without life, for example inside meteorites.
        • Maybe not from Mars. In this instrument some benzene can come from the rover’s own equipment. Proving a chemical is Martian takes more evidence.
        • Some bumps come from Earth. A lab chemical the rover carries (MTBSTFA) had soaked into this sample and makes bumps of its own.
        • One rock, one test. This is a single experiment on a single rock sample.
